\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{Continuous Assessment in Remote Learning}

Continuous assessment represents a paradigm shift from traditional episodic evaluation toward ongoing, formative assessment practices that integrate seamlessly with the learning process. In remote learning contexts, this approach becomes particularly critical as it provides multiple touchpoints for gauging student progress and engagement \cite{Kumar2024}. Unlike summative assessments that capture performance at discrete moments, continuous assessment enables educators to track learning trajectories and provide timely interventions when necessary.

The transition to remote education has highlighted both the potential and challenges of continuous assessment implementation. Digital learning environments offer unprecedented opportunities for data collection through learning analytics, participation tracking, and real-time feedback mechanisms \cite{Anderson2022}. However, these same environments also introduce complexities related to authentic assessment, digital equity, and the need to maintain human connection in evaluation processes \cite{Martinez2022}.

Research indicates that effective continuous assessment in remote settings requires careful consideration of multiple factors including technology accessibility, student autonomy, and the balance between automated and human-mediated evaluation \cite{Chen2024}. The integration of multiple assessment modalities—from traditional assignments to peer reviews and self-reflection activities—creates a more comprehensive picture of student learning while distributing the evaluative load across multiple touchpoints.

\subsection{Competency-Based Evaluation and Its Limitations}

Competency-based education (CBE) has gained significant traction in higher education as institutions seek to align learning outcomes with practical skills and measurable abilities. However, traditional implementations of CBE often rely on binary or discrete rating scales that fail to capture the nuanced nature of competency development \cite{Brown2023}. The conventional 0-1-2-3 scale, while administratively convenient, creates artificial boundaries that may not reflect actual learning progression.

\textbf{Problems of Subjectivity in Competency Measurement:} The fundamental limitation of discrete competency scales lies in their inability to represent partial mastery or progress toward competency achievement. Consider the common mapping where scores 0-59\% → Level 0, 60-69\% → Level 1, 70-84\% → Level 2, and 85-100\% → Level 3. Students scoring 69\% and 70\% receive different competency levels despite minimal performance difference, while students scoring 70\% and 84\% receive identical ratings despite substantial performance gaps. This threshold-based mapping introduces significant subjectivity, as instructors must make binary decisions about borderline cases without systematic support for such determinations \cite{Garcia2024}.

The fundamental limitation of discrete competency scales lies in their inability to represent partial mastery or progress toward competency achievement. Students who demonstrate substantial improvement but fall slightly short of predefined thresholds may receive the same rating as those showing minimal effort or understanding. This limitation becomes particularly problematic in remote learning environments where assessment must account for diverse learning contexts and varying levels of technological support \cite{Garcia2024}.

Moreover, competency-based assessment in traditional frameworks often struggles with the subjective nature of certain skills, particularly those related to critical thinking, collaboration, and communication. The rigid structure of discrete scales provides limited room for capturing the complexity of these competencies, potentially leading to oversimplified or misleading evaluations of student capabilities \cite{Johnson2023}.

\subsection{Fuzzy Logic in Educational Contexts}

Fuzzy logic provides a mathematical framework for reasoning with uncertain, imprecise, or subjective information—characteristics that are inherent in educational assessment contexts. Developed by Lotfi Zadeh in the 1960s, fuzzy logic extends classical binary logic by allowing for degrees of membership between 0 and 1, enabling more nuanced representation of real-world phenomena \cite{Santos2022}.

The application of fuzzy logic in educational settings typically involves four key components: fuzzification, rule base construction, inference mechanisms, and defuzzification \cite{Wang2023}. Fuzzification transforms crisp input values (such as assignment scores or participation rates) into fuzzy sets using membership functions that define degrees of belonging to linguistic categories. The rule base captures expert knowledge through IF-THEN statements that relate input conditions to output consequences. Inference mechanisms combine multiple rules to derive conclusions, while defuzzification converts fuzzy outputs back to crisp values for practical implementation.

Recent research has demonstrated the effectiveness of fuzzy logic approaches in various educational applications, including student performance prediction, adaptive learning systems, and assessment automation \cite{Smith2023}. These studies consistently show that fuzzy systems can provide more nuanced and fair evaluations compared to traditional binary approaches, particularly when dealing with subjective or multidimensional assessment criteria \cite{Nguyen2020}.

The integration of fuzzy logic into continuous assessment frameworks offers particular advantages for remote learning environments. By accommodating uncertainty and providing transparent reasoning processes, fuzzy systems can help educators make more informed decisions while maintaining the human element essential to effective teaching and learning \cite{Lopez2021, Black2020}.
